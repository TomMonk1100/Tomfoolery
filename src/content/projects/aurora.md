Kp is one number with an outsized reputation. It is a 3-hour, quasi-logarithmic planetary index built from ground magnetometers at 13 observatories, produced in definitive form by GFZ Potsdam, and NOAA SWPC maps it onto its public storm scale — Kp 5 is a G1 minor storm, Kp 9 a G5 extreme. The page lets you turn that dial from 0 to 9 and watch a seeded curtain field respond through a monotonic mapping in a tested pure core — brightness, coverage, ray count, motion, red oxygen tops at storm levels and the purple nitrogen lower edge — while a latitude strip tracks the oval's equatorward boundary against reference places from Fairbanks to southern Texas.

The boundary is the sourced part, and it is stated as what it is. NOAA SWPC's aurora viewing tips give an approximate average rule in geomagnetic latitude — about 66 degrees at Kp 0, moving equatorward about 2 degrees per Kp step to about 48 degrees at Kp 9 — and the page's model is exactly that line, no more. The colours trace to named emissions, green oxygen at 557.7 nm around 100–150 km, red oxygen at 630.0 nm above roughly 200 km, and ionised nitrogen at the lower edge, sourced from NOAA and the University of Alaska Fairbanks Geophysical Institute. The caveat is printed on the page in plain sight — this is a visual model driven by what the real Kp scale means, not a forecast. It makes no network calls, holds no live data, and says nothing about tonight.
