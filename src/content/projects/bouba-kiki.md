Show most people a rounded blob and a spiky star and ask which one is "bouba" and which is "kiki", and they agree — without being taught, and largely across languages. Wolfgang Köhler reported the pattern in 1929 with the words takete and maluma, publishing no counts at all. Ramachandran and Hubbard revived it in 2001 with bouba and kiki, reporting 95–98% agreement among US undergraduates and Tamil speakers, though without a sample size for that figure. The page runs the test on you: the classic pair first, then ten rounds where both the shapes and the two-syllable words are generated from seeded code — shapes from a single spikiness parameter, words from two disjoint sound families, round (b, m, l, n with oo/o/u/a) and sharp (k, t, p, s with ee/i/e).

The comparison at the end uses the strongest source, not the famous one. Ćwiek and colleagues (2022, Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B) tested 917 speakers of 25 languages with the words heard aloud and a strict both-trials rule, and found 72% matching overall — reliably above chance in 17 of the 25 languages, not all of them, with Romanian, Turkish and Mandarin Chinese below 50% descriptively. That is the honest shape of the finding: a real statistical tendency, not a universal law.

The caveat is built into the scoring, not footnoted away. Ten rounds for one person proves nothing — matching 8 of 10 still carries a two-sided binomial p of about 0.109 against chance, and the page computes and prints your own p-value next to your score. Your task also differs from the published one (written invented words, per-round choices), so the page says plainly that the numbers sit side by side for scale, not as a like-for-like comparison. It is labelled what it is: a toy replication you run on yourself — not a study.
