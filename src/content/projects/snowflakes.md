Ukichiro Nakaya grew snow crystals in a Hokkaido cold chamber in the 1930s and published the map in 1954: habit as a function of temperature and supersaturation. Kenneth Libbrecht's refinements at Caltech (SnowCrystals.com, the Snow Crystal Primer, American Scientist 2007) keep the same strange shape — thin plates near −2 °C, slender columns and needles near −5 °C, hollow columns near −7 °C, plates and stars again near −15 °C, and a plates-and-columns mix in the deep cold. The page draws that chart with a live marker, names the regime at your sliders, and prints where the sources disagree: the cold mixed zone starts at about −22 °C in the American Scientist diagram but "below −30 °C" in Libbrecht's review, and this page uses −22 °C and says so.

Next to the chart, a seeded frontier-growth model on a hexagonal lattice grows a crystal whose parameters are set by the regime — compact and filled for a plate, elongated for a needle, tip-hungry and sparse for a dendrite. It is labelled on the page as what it is: a stylised illustration informed by the real diagram, not Reiter's cellular model and not a physics simulation. What is tested is the part that can be tested — the regime table at probe points across the map, determinism per seed, and growth invariants over long runs, 120 checks in the gate. The sixfold symmetry needs no model at all: ice Ih locks water molecules into a hexagonal lattice, and the crystal inherits the six.
