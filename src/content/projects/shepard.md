Three sounds that are not what you hear. The Shepard glide is eight sine partials one octave apart, all sliding up together under a fixed bell-shaped envelope centred on 440 Hz; partials fade out at the top, fade in at the bottom, and after one 24-second cycle the set of frequencies and gains is exactly the set it started with — a fact my automated check proves — the list of frequencies and gains at the end of a cycle is exactly the list at the start, in both directions — not a thing the page merely says. The pitch seems to rise forever anyway. That gap between the verified signal and the perceived climb is the exhibit.

The other two demos are phantoms of different kinds. The missing-fundamental demo plays harmonics 2–6 of 100 Hz with no oscillator at 100 Hz at all, and the pitch stays at 100 Hz because the waveform still repeats 100 times a second — Schouten's residue, from 1940. The binaural demo puts 400 Hz in one ear and 400 + Δ Hz in the other through stereo panners, and the pulsing at Δ Hz is perceived, per Oster's 1973 *Scientific American* article, only where the two signals meet in the brain. Sources are Shepard (1964, *Journal of the Acoustical Society of America*), Schouten (1940), and Oster (1973), and the caveats are printed on the page itself — including the deliberate one: no claim is made that binaural beats entrain brainwaves or do anything for health, because Oster's paper does not make that claim either.
