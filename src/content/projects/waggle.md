Karl von Frisch decoded the dance in *The Dance Language and Orientation of Bees* (1967): on the vertical comb in the dark, up stands in for the sun, so the waggle run's angle from vertical copies the angle between the food and the sun's azimuth, and the run's duration carries the distance. The page gives you both directions — place food on a map and watch the bee dance it, or set a dance angle and duration and read out where the food is — with a still diagram holding the comb view and the sky view side by side so the copied angle is visible, not just asserted.

The calibration is printed on the page and hedged there too: the slope is von Frisch's, roughly 75 ms of waggle per 100 m for his Carniolan bees, with a 250 ms intercept chosen so the line passes through Seeley's rule of thumb of about one second per kilometre (*The Wisdom of the Hive*, 1995). It is not a universal constant — subspecies run different dialects, and the modern revision is stranger than the original decoding: Srinivasan and colleagues (*Science*, 2000) showed the bee's odometer integrates optic flow, not ground distance, so six metres flown through a narrow patterned tunnel were danced as roughly 190 metres. What the dance encodes is the flight as the bee's eyes experienced it.
