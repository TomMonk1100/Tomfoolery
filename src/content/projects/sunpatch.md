Sunpatch grew out of a design question: what does a deck-building roguelite look like with no poker hands and no enemies? The answer is a garden. You plant seed cards in a nine-plot bed; every plant's value climbs stage by stage — 1, 3, 6 — peaks under a gold ring, then fades. Score comes from *when* you pick, not what you play.

The week is telegraphed like a weather report: this week's sky and the next two are printed on screen, and Sun, Water and Moon plants grow an extra stage when their weather lands — which can push a tomato straight past its peak if you weren't watching the forecast. Neighbours matter quietly: a sunflower feeds the plants beside it, a pumpkin wants the whole bed to itself, a chili doubles in a corner. Every bonus is already inside the number printed on the tile; there is no hidden maths anywhere in the game.

Seasons are quotas — Spring asks 90, Winter 190 — and anything past the quota turns into coins for the seed market between seasons, where tools wait: a watering can, a beehive, a glasshouse, and a black cat named Theo who naps on one plant a week and supervises it into +3. The touches of home are seasoning, not the meal: a Slowpoke Melon that takes all season to be worth 16, a Desert Agave for the Texas heat, catnip for the cat.

The design follows a researched rulebook — one verb, every outcome previewed before you commit, a single score, the quota never off screen. Thirty engine tests and four hundred bot-grown gardens (15% grew a full year) keep the quotas honest, and every run is seeded: same seed, same weather, garden differently.
