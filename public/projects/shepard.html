Explain it like I'm 5

Your ears can be fooled in honest ways. One sound seems to climb up and up forever, like stairs that never get higher — but it is going in a circle. One sound is a low note you can hear even though that note was never played. And if each ear hears a slightly different tone, you hear a slow pulsing that is not in either ear's sound at all. Three tricks, and every one of them is your hearing doing its normal job on purpose-built sounds.

Why you're looking at this

I like illusions where the trick is fully visible and still works. Nothing on this page is hidden: the diagram shows the partials sliding under the envelope while the endless-rise sound plays, the missing-fundamental demo lists every frequency it actually plays, and the binaural demo shows both ear frequencies on screen. I checked the maths behind all three with a test gate before building the sounds — including proving that after one full glide the Shepard tone is literally the same set of frequencies and gains it started with. It does not rise. That is the point, and it still sounds like it does.

1 · the shepard glide — rising forever, going nowhere

Roger Shepard's 1964 paper in the Journal of the Acoustical Society of America, "Circularity in judgments of relative pitch," built complex tones that break the idea of pitch as a straight line: each tone could be judged higher than the one before it, all the way around a circle. The construction, as described in the Psychology of Music auditory demonstrations: components separated by octaves, their amplitudes scaled by a fixed, bell-shaped spectral envelope — loudest in the middle of the range, quietest at the extremes — with all components shifted up or down in log-frequency.

This demo glides continuously instead of stepping in semitones (Shepard's originals stepped; the envelope trick is the same). Eight sine partials (a partial is one of the stacked tones that make the sound), exactly one octave apart, all glide up together. The bell envelope — drawn below, centred on 440 Hz — does not move. A partial climbing toward the top fades out; a new one fades in at the bottom. After 24 seconds each partial has climbed exactly one octave, and the set of frequencies and gains is exactly the set it started with. That return is not a slogan: my automated check proves the list of frequencies and gains at the end of a cycle is exactly the list at the start. The sound does not really rise. It circles, and your pitch sense follows the circle.

2 · the missing fundamental — a pitch with no tone at it

Play a harmonic complex tone — a fundamental plus its whole-number multiples — and you hear one pitch: the fundamental. Now remove the fundamental and keep the multiples. In 1940 J. F. Schouten showed that cancelling the fundamental of a pitched tone did not change its pitch, and he named the surviving pitch the residue. His experiments also showed the missing fundamental is not being re-created inside the ear by distortion in any appreciable amount; he pointed at the periodicity of the waveform instead. That fits the arithmetic: 200, 300, 400, 500 and 600 Hz are harmonics 2–6 of 100 Hz, their greatest common divisor is still 100, and the summed wave still repeats 100 times a second — even though nothing in it vibrates at 100 Hz.

both versions are built to be heard at the same pitch — 100 Hz. only the first one contains any 100 Hz.

3 · binaural beats — a pulse that is in neither ear

You must use headphones for this one — speakers will not work. Gerald Oster's 1973 Scientific American article "Auditory beats in the brain" describes what happens when tones of different frequency are presented separately to each ear: a slow modulation — a binaural beat — is perceived. Ordinary beats, heard when two tones mix in the air, pulse at the difference between the two frequencies. Binaural beats are different in kind: each ear receives one steady pure tone, nothing pulses in either channel, and the pulsing is perceived as a consequence of the two signals being combined in the brain. Oster notes binaural beats need low carrier tones — best perceived around 440 Hz, less distinct above that, and gone above about 1,000 Hz — which is why this demo's base tone is 400 Hz.

left ear 400 Hz · right ear 406 Hz · perceived beat 6 Hz. the beat is perceived only — neither channel contains it.

What this page claims about binaural beats is only that: a beat at the difference frequency is perceived. Full stop. See the notes for what is deliberately not claimed.

notes

  • Sources: Shepard, R. N. (1964), "Circularity in judgments of relative pitch," Journal of the Acoustical Society of America 36(12), 2346–2353 (doi 10.1121/1.1919362); Schouten, J. F. (1940), "The residue, a new component in subjective sound analysis," Proceedings of the Koninklijke Nederlandsche Akademie van Wetenschappen 43(3), 356–365; Oster, G. (1973), "Auditory beats in the brain," Scientific American 229(4), 94–102. Oster credits the discovery of binaural beats to H. W. Dove in 1839.
  • The Shepard tone does not really rise or fall without limit. After one octave of glide the sound is bit-for-bit the same recipe it started as. The page says this while the illusion is playing, because the illusion surviving the explanation is the interesting part.
  • This page makes no claim that binaural beats entrain brainwaves, heal anything, improve focus or sleep, or have any other health effect. Oster's 1973 article is about how the auditory system processes the two signals, and about the beat as a research and diagnostic tool. The health-product claims attached to binaural beats elsewhere are not in that paper and are not made here.
  • Shepard's 1964 tones stepped in semitones; this demo glides continuously (a glissando form of the same construction). Schouten's own examples used a 200 Hz fundamental; this demo uses 100 Hz so the with/without contrast is easy to hear — the choice of 100 Hz is this page's, not his.
  • Design parameters of this synthesis, stated as design parameters and not as facts from the papers: 8 partials from a 55 Hz base, a Gaussian envelope of width 1.1 octaves centred on 440 Hz, a 24-second glide cycle, harmonics 1–6 (with) and 2–6 (without) at gains 1/n, and a 400 Hz binaural base with Δ from 1–30 Hz (6 Hz default — the difference in Oster's own worked example, 440 Hz against 434 Hz).
  • Perception varies between listeners, headphones, and rooms. The gate verifies the signals, not your experience of them; if a demo sounds weak, the likeliest suspects are small speakers (demo 2) and speakers instead of headphones (demo 3, where the two tones mix in the air and you get ordinary monaural beats instead).
  • Everything is synthesised live from the same pure functions the gate tested — octave spacing, envelope gains, partial lists, and the binaural pair are computed, not recorded or hard-coded per demo.
