Explain it like I'm 5

Before sunrise, birds start singing one after another, like an orchestra warming up. This page does not play recordings. It builds little fake bird phrases out of pure computer tones, following the recipe books bird scientists wrote down — this bird repeats the same short tune, that bird whistles only two notes — and it draws a picture of the sound while it plays.

Why you're looking at this

I wanted to know how much of a birdsong is its structure and how much is the voice itself. So I kept only the structure — the counts, the groupings, the trill in the middle — and threw the voice away, replacing it with one plain oscillator. What is left is built so the phrases are tellable apart — two-note sparrow against teakettle wren — without any real bird recording. That felt worth testing honestly, with the limits printed on the page.

American robin Turdus migratorius

Sourced description: a string of about 10 clear whistles from a few repeated syllables, in 4–8 carolling "cheerily, cheer up" phrases, pitch rising and falling, with pauses between phrases. (Cornell Lab Bird Academy)

Song sparrow Melospiza melodia

Sourced description: a few clear introductory notes, then a trill, then varied buzzes and notes — "Maids! Maids! Maids! Put on your teakettle-ettle-ettle", about 2–4 seconds. (Cornell Lab All About Birds; Maine Audubon)

White-throated sparrow Zonotrichia albicollis

Sourced description: pure whistled notes on distinct frequencies — "Old Sam Peabody, Peabody, Peabody" — two single notes, then triplets, typically on two pitches. This build models the classic triplet form only. (Cornell Lab Bird Academy, Handbook of Bird Biology visualizations)

Carolina wren Thryothorus ludovicianus

Sourced description: loud, rolling, repeated phrases — several quick whistled notes, usually three-parted, repeated a few times: "teakettle-teakettle-teakettle". Only males sing. (Cornell Lab All About Birds)

notes

  • These are synthesized imitations modelled on published descriptions of each song's structure — not recordings of real birds, and the spectrogram shows the synthesis, drawn live from an AnalyserNode.
  • No numeric pitch range was sourced from the Cornell or Xeno-canto text descriptions used here, so no Hz value on this page is data. The oscillator pitches (roughly 2,600–4,800 Hz across species) are the synth's choice, picked to keep the four phrases apart to the ear.
  • Each press varies the phrase slightly, from a seeded generator — the same seed always gives the same phrase in the tested core, and the page advances the seed per performance, the way a real bird varies a little between renditions. The structure rules do not vary.
  • Gate: the pure core (no DOM, no AudioContext) was tested in Node over 20 seeds per species — 2,296 checks passed, 0 failed — covering determinism per seed, no syllable overlapping itself in time, the white-throated sparrow using at most 2 distinct base pitches, the song sparrow trill holding at least 6 rapid syllables, robin grouping and pauses, and the wren motif repeating in threes.
  • The full-chorus stagger is a presentation choice. No source was used to claim a fixed order in which these species sing at a real dawn, and this page does not claim one.
  • Sources: Cornell Lab Bird Academy, American Robin lesson; Cornell Lab All About Birds overviews and sounds pages for Song Sparrow, White-throated Sparrow and Carolina Wren; Cornell Lab, Avian Vocal Behavior sound visualizations (Handbook of Bird Biology); Maine Audubon, Song Sparrow profile.
