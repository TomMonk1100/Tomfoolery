Explain it like I'm 5

Same blinking fireflies, but this time we poke them — change how strongly they copy each other — and watch whether the group still agrees on one rhythm or falls apart.

Why you're looking at this

Fireflies showed me synchrony happens. Pulseflies is me asking how much it takes to break it. I like knowing where a pattern's edge is, not just that the pattern exists.

pulseflies — pulse-coupled oscillators

Each firefly has a phase that advances at its own natural frequency (about one flash per second, with a 12% spread). When a firefly's phase reaches 1 it flashes, resets to 0, and kicks each neighbor within a 0.25 radius: the neighbor's phase grows by ε · phase. The kick grows with phase, so an oscillator near threshold gets pushed over it (absorption — the flash drags its neighbors with it), while a just-fired oscillator at phase 0 is immune. This is the Mirollo–Strogatz mechanism: real fireflies pulse-couple, they don't phase-couple. Nothing is hand-synced; synchrony either emerges from the kicks or it doesn't.

What the sweep found (measured, not tuned)

Pulse coupling syncs a local lattice where phase coupling didn't. Last night's Kuramoto local mode reached only r = 0.424 at K = 1.9 (dashed line); pulse coupling reaches r = 0.947 at ε = 0.05, with flash coincidence C = 0.988. The transition is sharp: r jumps from 0.335 to 0.841 between ε = 0.025 and 0.03. In the transition zone the two metrics disagree — r dips (0.402 → 0.335) while C climbs (0.142 → 0.284) from ε = 0.02 to 0.025: flash clusters form before phase alignment does. Above the transition, r saturates near 0.954 while C saturates at 1.000: flashes coincide exactly (absorption re-clusters the lattice every cycle), but phases spread between flashes because natural frequencies differ, so r caps below 1. No traveling waves: the mean neighbor phase difference is ~0 at every ε — the intermediate regime is partial clustering, not a wave. Multi-seed check: the transition is real, its address is not a point

The sweep above is one seed. Re-running the same grid on six seeds (6 × 13 runs, N = 120, 120 s each) shows the transition in every seed — all six reach r ≥ 0.8 by ε = 0.05 — but the crossing address moves: first ε with r ≥ 0.8 is 0.0275, 0.03, 0.03, 0.03, 0.035, and 0.05 across the six seeds (median 0.03, span 0.0225). The single-seed claim "between 0.025 and 0.03" is therefore corrected to a range: the transition sits in 0.0275–0.05 depending on the draw, typically at 0.03. Five seeds jump sharply; one (seed 20261026) climbs gradually (0.634 → 0.834 across 0.0225–0.05) with no sharp jump anywhere — gradual transitions exist in this system, they are just the minority. A bigger-lattice spot check (N = 240, same radius, seed 20251025) syncs even earlier — r = 0.882 already at ε = 0.02, against 0.402 at N = 120 — but doubling N at fixed radius also doubles the mean neighbor count, so this spot check confounds size with density: the direction (denser syncs easier) is established, the attribution is not. Dots mark each seed's first r ≥ 0.8 crossing.

Size vs density: the big-lattice boost is density, not size

The spot check above confounded two causes, so a six-arm experiment separated them (3 seeds each, N and radius and neighbor thinning varied independently; mean neighbor counts measured, not assumed). At ε = 0.02, mean steady r across seeds: baseline A (N = 120) 0.355; big lattice B (N = 240, same radius) 0.903 — the gap is 0.548 and it reproduces in all three seeds. Doubling the size with the degree held fixed gives no boost: arm C2 (N = 240, neighbors thinned to the baseline degree) 0.366, and arm C1 (N = 240, radius shrunk to hold the degree) 0.274. Doubling the degree at fixed size reproduces the boost almost exactly: arm D (N = 120, radius grown) 0.878, which is 0.95 of the gap. Extending the interaction range with the degree held fixed gives no boost either: arm D2 0.255. Measured mean degrees: A 18.4, B 36.9, C1 19.8, C2 18.5, D 32.9, D2 16.5 — the fixed-degree arms sit within 15% of baseline, so the comparison is fair. The attribution holds seed by seed, not just on average (density effect 0.50– 0.55, size effect −0.05–0.10, range effect −0.13–−0.06 in every seed). More neighbors per firefly sync the lattice earlier; more fireflies do not. Above the transition the arms converge (all ≥ 0.90 at ε = 0.05): density moves the transition, it does not raise the ceiling. Thinning is symmetric — each kept pair is kept in both directions — and is a new opt-in core knob (neighborKeep), off by default, so the live toy above is unchanged.
