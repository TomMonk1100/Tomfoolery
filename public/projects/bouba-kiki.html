Explain it like I'm 5

Here are two shapes. One is round and blobby, one is spiky. Here are two made-up words: "bouba" and "kiki". Most people — in most languages tested — say the round one is bouba, and the spiky one is kiki. Nobody taught them that. The sounds just feel like they fit the shapes. This page lets you test whether your brain does it too, with new shapes and new made-up words the computer invents on the spot.

Why you're looking at this

I like this effect because it is a crack in a tidy idea. The tidy idea says the sound of a word has nothing to do with what it means — any sound could name anything. Bouba/kiki says that is mostly true and not completely true, and the honest numbers are more interesting than the famous ones: the headline 95–98% comes from a paper that never printed its sample size, and the biggest careful test, 917 people in 25 languages, landed at 72% — real, robust, and nowhere near universal. So I built the test so you run it on the one subject you have full access to, with the caveats printed on the page instead of buried.

what the published studies found

Three waypoints. Köhler published no numbers at all. Ramachandran & Hubbard reported a very high rate without a sample size. Ćwiek and colleagues ran the big, careful version — heard words, shapes one at a time, and a strict rule: you only count as matching if you get both words right.

The Ćwiek detail that matters: the effect was reliably above chance in 17 of the 25 languages — not all of them. Romanian, Turkish and Mandarin Chinese speakers sat below 50% descriptively. Bouba-to-round held in 22 of 25 languages; kiki-to-spiky in only 11. A real tendency, varying by language. Not a law.

notes

  • 10 rounds for one person proves nothing. If you matched 8 of the 10 novel rounds, a coin-flipper does at least that well (in either direction) about 11% of the time — two-sided binomial p ≈ 0.109, computed on this page. Even 10/10 only reaches p ≈ 0.002 for a sample of one person's one sitting. The published samples are hundreds of people; yours is a demonstration, not evidence.
  • Your score is not directly comparable to the 72% above. Ćwiek's participants heard the words spoken and had to get both trials right; you read invented words and choose per round. Different task, different number.
  • The 95–98% figure is quoted as reported. The 2001 paper does not print a sample size for it in the passage everyone cites, so treat it as a striking claim with thin documentation, not a precision measurement.
  • Köhler's 1929 words were takete and maluma — no counts, just an invitation to the reader, who he expected would "decide with ease". Retellings that say "baluba" for 1929 are repeating a slip.
  • Why it happens is unsettled. Candidates: the shape of the mouth and lips, the acoustic sharpness of the consonants, and — for readers — the shapes of the letters themselves. Ćwiek's audio design shows letter shape is not the whole story. This page shows words in writing, so letter shape is in play here.
  • How the toy works: shapes are seeded polygons driven by one spikiness parameter (in the test gate, spiky shapes average 4.7× the radial variance of round ones). Words are built from two disjoint letter families — round: b, m, l, n + oo/o/u/a; sharp: k, t, p, s + ee/i/e — so a generated word is never a blend. Same seed, same shapes and words, every visit — unless you take the new-seed replay at the end, which generates a fresh set.
  • Sources: Köhler, Gestalt Psychology (1929); Ramachandran & Hubbard, Journal of Consciousness Studies 8(12), 2001; Ćwiek et al., Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B 377:20200390, 2022 (doi:10.1098/rstb.2020.0390); cross-checked against the Science news report of 2021-11-15 and Cuskley, Simner & Kirby (2017).
