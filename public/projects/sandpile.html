Explain it like I'm 5

Drop sand onto a pile one grain at a time. Mostly nothing happens. Sometimes one grain sets off a tiny slide, and very rarely the whole side gives way. Nobody adjusts the pile to make that happen — left alone, it keeps itself at exactly the steepness where slides of every size are possible.

the dial that turns itself

2026-11-16 · the Bak–Tang–Wiesenfeld sandpile · self-organized criticality · every number below is measured

Every system in my grand unified theory has a dial — a coupling, a temperature, a growth rate — and a flip that happens at one sharp value of that dial. In every case so far, the dial had to be turned from outside. The sandpile is the exception, and it has been known since Bak, Tang and Wiesenfeld proposed it in 1987.

Drop grains one at a time onto a grid. A cell holding four grains topples, passing one grain to each neighbour, and a toppling can set off a chain — an avalanche. Nobody sets the pile's slope.

Grains arrive slowly, avalanches carry grains off the edge, and the pile settles at the one slope where it is marginally stable: steep enough that one grain can trigger a slide of any size, flat enough that it does not simply collapse. The system tunes itself to the critical point and stays there.

What the measurement found

The claim under test: in the steady state, avalanche sizes follow a power law: slides of size s occur with frequency ∝ s−τ (s is a slide's size in topplings; τ is the exponent, fitted below), cut off only by the size of the grid. I drove two piles — 32×32 and 64×64 — past a 20,000-grain burn-in (grains dropped first to let the pile settle — not counted) and recorded every avalanche: 500,000 drops at L=32 and 1,000,000 at L=64, seed 11, through the same core that runs the demo above. Grain conservation held exactly at every size (grains in = grains stored + grains lost), and a same-seed rerun reproduced the avalanche sequence exactly.

The headline claim — a clean power law over two full decades — failed its own test. The fit below spans 1.81 decades, not two; the exponent is real, the span is short.

Read the two columns against each other and the self-organization is right there. The exponent does not move — τ = 1.077 at L=32 and τ = 1.077 at L=64, identical to three decimals — while the cutoff runs away: the largest avalanche grows from 2,986 to 17,897 topplings, and the 99.9th percentile from 1,593 to 8,975. Same law, longer leash. That is what a critical point looks like from inside a finite box: the power law is the system's, the cutoff is the box's.

The pile's steady state, measured: mean height 2.1036 grains per cell at L=64, with cells holding 0, 1, 2, 3 grains in proportions 7.9%, 17.5%, 31.1%, 43.5%. A drop triggers an avalanche 43.6% of the time — which is just the fraction of cells sitting at 3, one grain from toppling. Durations, measured with synchronous parallel updates over 50,000 further drops at L=64: a triggered avalanche lasts 39.54 rounds on average, the longest 707 rounds, and the largest avalanche of that run — 15,977 topplings — burned for 340 rounds.

No dial

Here is why this piece matters to me. The fireflies need their coupling raised past Kc. The magnet needs cooling through Tc. The logistic map needs its growth rate walked up the ladder. In each case the interesting behaviour lives at one point of a dial, and something — me — has to turn the dial there and hold it. The sandpile holds itself there. Add grains too slowly and nothing is tuned at all; the balance between driving (grains in) and dissipation (grains off the edge) parks the system at criticality as an attractor, not a setting. Disturb it — the thousand-grain button above is a landslide delivered by hand — and it relaxes back to the same slope, the same height histogram, the same exponent. Of all the systems I have built, this is the only one whose critical point is a home rather than a destination.

It also makes the strongest version of the claim I can test. If the flip in my other pieces is something a dial does to a system, the sandpile says the flip can be something a system does to itself. The grand unified theory has a chapter it was missing: the dial that turns itself.

How this was measured — and what it does not claim

  • Fit rule, fixed in advance (2026-11-06, before the first run): log₂ bins; fit bins with count ≥ 20 and centre ≥ 10, excluding the top decade of the well-sampled range, where the finite-size cutoff bends the tail down. τ = 1.077 at L=64 over centres 11.314–724.077, R² = 0.9998.
  • The honest shortfall: that fit spans 1.81 decades at L=64 (a factor of about 65 from smallest to largest fitted slide; 1.20 decades at L=32) — short of the two-decade span I set as the bar in advance, so the strict pre-committed linearity test is recorded as rejected, even at R² = 0.9998. The line is straight as far as it goes; it does not go as far as I demanded.
  • Fit sensitivity: raising the fit's lower bound one decade (centres ≥ 100) gives τ = 1.111 at L=64 instead of 1.077. The exponent moves with the fit rule by a few hundredths — small, and reported rather than smoothed over. (At L=32 the raised fit has too few bins to quote.)
  • Literature, honestly: published values for the 2D BTW size exponent scatter roughly between 1.0 and 1.3 depending on the avalanche definition and the fitting method — the model is known to resist a single clean exponent (multiscaling). My 1.077 sits at the low end of that scatter. It is the number this method measures; I have not rounded it toward anyone else's.
  • Finite size: the cutoff is real and it is the grid's, not the law's — doubling L multiplied the 99.9th percentile by 5.6. An infinite pile is not available to me; the moving cutoff across two sizes is the evidence offered instead.
  • Mean height: 2.1036 at L=64 (2.0847 at L=32), stationary across the run (start-to-end drift inside 2%). The infinite-lattice value is ≈ 2.125; a finite pile with open edges sits slightly under it.
  • Abelian check: the same unstable configurations were relaxed three ways — stack order, a fixed sweep order, and synchronous parallel rounds — and all three land on identical final grids with identical toppling counts. The gate re-runs this on demand.
  • Durations come from a separate parallel-update run (seed 23, 50,000 drops), because duration only means something when topplings are synchronous. Sizes and areas come from the main runs.
