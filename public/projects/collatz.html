Collatz

Every number anyone has ever tested falls to 1. Nobody knows why.

Take any whole number. If it is even, halve it. If it is odd, triple it and add 1. Repeat. The claim — unproved since 1937 — is that you always reach 1, no matter where you start. Some numbers take the long way down.

n → n/2  (n even)   |   n → 3n+1  (n odd)

The pattern in the averages

One trajectory tells you almost nothing. So I counted every starting number up to N and asked: what share of all steps are odd steps? A fair coin would say one half. The measured share sits just below one half, and closes on it slowly, in a very specific way:

π(N) ≈ ½ − 0.0496 / ln N

Green dots are measured values — every starting number from 1 to N, counted exactly. The amber line is the fit. The value at N = 1010 was predicted from the fit before the count ran, and landed within 0.00002 of it.

What I have found so far

  • The deficit lives at the end. Split each trajectory into head, middle, and tail. The last ten steps run odd-poor — the only road into 1 is 8 → 4 → 2 → 1, and that forced ending accounts for a deficit of about 1.69 odd steps per starting number.
  • The middle runs odd-rich. The middle of a trajectory has an odd share of about 0.516 — above the fair-coin line. It gives back about 1.37 odd steps per start. That surplus is measured, not explained. It is the open half of the problem.
  • Why the law looks like 1/ln N. A near-constant tail deficit, minus a slowly shrinking middle surplus, spread over trajectories whose average length grows like ln N, produces exactly a 1/ln N approach to one half. The shape of the law falls out of the split. Deriving the constant 0.0496 from the mechanism — rather than fitting it — is the step that would count as a breakthrough.
  • A pattern in averages is not a proof. The conjecture stays open. Computers have verified every starting number beyond 270, and that is still not a proof, for the same reason this page is not one.
