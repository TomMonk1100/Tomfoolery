Explain it like I'm 5

A snowflake's shape is a weather report from the cloud it grew in. Two things decide it: how cold the air was, and how much extra water vapour was floating around. Warm-ish cold (just below freezing) makes flat six-sided plates. A bit colder makes long thin needles. Colder still makes plates again — and at about −15 °C with lots of vapour, the corners race ahead and you get the big feathery stars from Christmas cards. Every crystal has six sides because ice itself is built on a six-sided pattern, all the way down to its molecules.

Why you're looking at this

I like a map that fits on one page and still embarrasses physics. Ukichiro Nakaya drew this one in the 1930s by growing crystals in a cold room, and the strange part is still unexplained: as the air gets colder the habit flips plate, column, plate again, like the crystal cannot make up its mind. Nobody fully knows why. The chart below is that real map, with the sources named and the disagreements printed. The growing crystal next to it is my illustration of the map's regimes — honest about being an illustration, which is more than most snowflake pictures manage.

why six

Ordinary ice (ice Ih) locks water molecules into a hexagonal lattice, and a snow crystal inherits that lattice's sixfold symmetry as it grows — so it grows six arms, never five or seven. That is the whole explanation, and it is enough: the six is in the crystal structure, not in the weather. The weather only decides what the six arms do.

notes

  • Sources: Ukichiro Nakaya, Snow Crystals: Natural and Artificial (Harvard University Press, 1954), from his 1930s cold-chamber work at Hokkaido University; Kenneth G. Libbrecht, "Guide to Snowflakes" and "A Snow Crystal Primer", SnowCrystals.com / Caltech (snowcrystals.com/morphology/morphology.html); Libbrecht, "The Formation of Snow Crystals", American Scientist 95 (2007); Libbrecht's morphology review (plates near −2 °C, columns and needles near −5 °C, hollow columns near −7 °C, plates again near −15 °C, columns again below −30 °C).
  • The anchors the sources agree on: thin plates near −2 °C, columns and slender needles near −5 °C, plates and stars again near −15 °C, simpler forms at low humidity and more complex, branched forms at high humidity at every temperature.
  • Where the sources differ, this page says so: Libbrecht's review puts "columns again" below −30 °C and his Primer puts a plates-and-columns mix "around −30 °C", while the American Scientist diagram puts the mixed plates-and-columns zone starting at about −22 °C. This page starts the mixed zone at −22 °C. The Guide also centres slender columns near −6 °C where the Primer says −5 °C; this page centres needles on −5 °C.
  • Boundaries are laboratory summaries, not sharp physical edges. A real falling crystal passes through changing air and can record several regimes in one crystal — that is how capped columns happen.
  • Why the habit flips plate → column → plate as temperature falls is, in Libbrecht's words, still something of a scientific mystery. This page does not pretend otherwise.
  • The growth visual is a seeded frontier-growth illustration on a hexagonal lattice. Its parameters (tip preference, fill preference, elongation) are set by the regime at your sliders. It is not Reiter's 2005 cellular model, not a diffusion solver, and not a prediction of any real crystal. Same settings and same seed always grow the identical crystal — that determinism is tested, along with the regime table, in the page's core gate (120 checks).
  • Try it: −2 °C at low humidity grows a compact hexagonal plate; −5 °C grows an elongated needle form (drawn in the plate plane, so read it as a habit sketch, not a view of a real needle); −15 °C at high humidity grows the fernlike dendrite.
