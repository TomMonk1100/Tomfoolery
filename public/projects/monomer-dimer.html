Explain it like I'm 5

Take a chessboard and punch holes in it — each hole is a square no domino can cover. How many ways are left to tile the rest? Sometimes the answer collapses to exactly zero, and you can see why without any hard maths: colour the board like a chessboard. Every domino always covers one light square and one dark square. So if the squares left over are not half light and half dark, no tiling can exist at all. Count the colours and you already know.

Why you're looking at this

The famous version is the mutilated chessboard: cut off two opposite corners and 31 dominoes can never cover the rest, because the two corners are the same colour. I wanted to poke that board myself — click squares, watch the exact count fall, sometimes to zero, sometimes to a number in the millions. Every count on this page is recomputed live by an exact counter after every click, and on small boards a second, completely separate counter checks it.

notes

  • Colour balance is a hard rule, not a hint: every domino covers exactly one light and one dark square, so the remaining squares must split evenly. If the readout above says the balance is not zero, the count must be 0 — and the counter will say 0.
  • The classic preset is the mutilated chessboard. The two opposite corners of an 8×8 board have the same colour, so removing them leaves 32 squares of one colour and 30 of the other. 31 dominoes would need 31 of each. Count: exactly 0.
  • Equal colour balance does not promise a tiling exists — other shapes can still block every tiling. Balance can prove zero; it cannot prove nonzero. The counter settles the nonzero cases.
  • Caveats, printed plainly: the DP counter's cost grows with board height (its state is a bitmask over the rows), so this page keeps boards at 8 rows or fewer. The brute-force counter is exponential and is only shown on boards up to 5×5, where it finishes quickly.
  • The tested core behind this page passed 172 checks, including the DP and brute-force counters agreeing on every holed board tested.
