Explain it like I'm 5

Your eye doesn't see the whole rainbow recipe of a light — it only takes three measurements. So two lights with completely different recipes can give the same three measurements, and then they look exactly the same colour. Different recipe, same taste. Those look-alike lights are called metamers, and it's the only reason a screen with just three colours of lamp can fool you into seeing every colour.

Why you're looking at this

I wanted to watch the collapse happen. A spectrum here has 81 numbers; a colour has 3. That 81-to-3 squeeze is done with the real CIE 1931 colour-matching functions, downloaded as a published table, not invented. The part I like: give the page a smooth, broad spectrum, and it will build you a completely different light — three thin spikes — that the standard observer cannot tell apart, and it prints the difference as a number, about 10−16. Not "looks close." The same, to machine precision, with a wildly different spectrum.

how the metamer is built

Three spikes have three unknown amplitudes, and a colour match is three equations (X, Y, Z) — so the page searches triples of wavelengths on a 10 nm grid from 400 to 700 nm, solves the 3×3 linear system for each triple exactly (the square case of least squares), and keeps the all-nonnegative solution with the smallest total amplitude. The result is not tuned until it looks right; it is solved. In the offline test gate, five different target colours all matched with ΔXYZ below 2×10−16 while the spectra differed by an L2 distance of 10–15 (the spectra themselves have L2 norms around 5–9, so the difference is larger than the thing itself).

notes

  • Data: CIE 1931 2° colour-matching functions from the Colour & Vision Research Laboratory (CVRL), file ciexyz31.csv, 5 nm steps; this page embeds the 380–780 nm slice (81 rows). cvrl.org was unreachable from my machine, so the identical file came from the University of Ottawa's mirror of the same table; I verified ȳ(555 nm) = 1 exactly and that the three column sums agree to ~0.03% before trusting it.
  • Convention, stated precisely: a spectrum here is the light — relative spectral power per bin. There is no separate illuminant and no reflecting object. XYZ = Σ S·(x̄, ȳ, z̄) / Σ ȳ, so a flat spectrum gives Y = 1 and X ≈ Z ≈ 1.
  • XYZ → sRGB uses the IEC 61966-2-1 matrix for the D65 white point, in the form published by Bruce Lindbloom, then the sRGB gamma curve. The gate checks that the D65 white point (0.95047, 1, 1.08883) converts to linear sRGB ≈ (1, 1, 1).
  • Out of gamut is flagged, not hidden: when a computed linear sRGB channel leaves [0, 1], the page says so under the swatch. The swatch colour itself is then the clipped display value — your screen physically cannot show the real colour.
  • The match is exact only for the CIE 1931 standard observer — an average built from a small group of 1920s observers, known to be off at short wavelengths (that is why the Judd and Judd–Vos corrections exist). Real observers vary; a metamer for the standard observer can be a visible mismatch for you.
  • Metamerism here is under this page's single fixed convention. Change the illuminant in the real world — daylight to tungsten — and two spectra that matched can split apart. Nothing on this page models that.
  • The spike spectra are solutions to a linear system on a 5 nm grid, not a recipe for a buildable lamp.
