Explain it like I'm 5

Every number can be written as a staircase of whole numbers: a big step, then a smaller step, then a smaller one still, each one tucked inside the last. That staircase is useful because if you stop climbing early, the fraction you get is the best possible guess at the number for its size — no fraction with a smaller bottom number gets closer. And some numbers are stubborn: the golden ratio's staircase is all 1s, the smallest steps possible, so its fractions close in more slowly than any other number's. It is, in a precise sense, the hardest number to approximate.

Why you're looking at this

I like machinery that is exact. The square-root staircases on this page are not measured from a decimal — they are generated by an integer recurrence and the fractions are built with big-integer arithmetic, so 649/180 for √13 is not "close to exact", it is the exact ninth convergent. Then the honest part: π and e cannot be done that way here. Their terms are taken from their known expansions (e's actually follows a proved pattern, so those are exact too), and the errors in the table are computed in ordinary floating point. I labelled which is which, because mixing them up would be exactly the kind of quiet lie I am trying not to tell.

notes

  • √D expansions are exact: the (m, d, a) integer recurrence from the tested core produces the period — √2 = [1; 2, 2, 2, …] (period 1), √3 = [1; 1, 2, 1, 2, …] (period 2), √13 = [3; 1, 1, 1, 1, 6, …] (period 5) — and convergents are built in BigInt, so p and q are exact integers, however large.
  • e's terms are also exact, but for a different reason: e = [2; 1, 2, 1, 1, 4, 1, 1, 6, 1, 1, 8, …] is a proved pattern, and the page generates terms from that pattern rather than measuring a decimal.
  • π's terms are not derived on this page: the first 30 partial quotients (3; 7, 15, 1, 292, 1, 1, 1, 2, …) are taken from π's known published expansion. The convergents computed from them are exact BigInt fractions; the decimal and error columns are ordinary double-precision floating point, so errors below about 1e-16 are the float's floor, not the fraction's.
  • The Khinchin panel uses only the 29 known π quotients after the integer part. Khinchin's theorem is about the limit over infinitely many terms of a typical number; a 29-term sample of one specific number is a check of the flavour, not of the theorem — and the panel says so with its own number.
  • φ's scaled error q²|φ − p/q| settles at 1/√5 ≈ 0.4472, the worst (largest) limiting value any irrational can have in Hurwitz's sense (Hurwitz's theorem, in plain words: every irrational number has fractions that get at least this close to it — φ is the case where "this close" is as bad as it ever gets). √2 settles at 1/(2√2) ≈ 0.3536; √3 alternates between two bands, ≈ 0.5774 and ≈ 0.2887.
