Explain it like I'm 5

Imagine a rumour in a school where everyone sits in a grid and only whispers to the four people right beside them. One number says how catching the rumour is. Below a magic value it dies out in a corner. Above it, it crosses the whole school. This page measures that magic value — and shows it is not quite the number the simple textbook formula gives.

Why you're looking at this

Of all my flips, this is the one that walks around in ordinary life. A spark and a fire are the same physics; one number crossing one line decides which of the two you get. I built the grid version because the honest measurement surprised me — space itself pushes back, and the crossing moves. The gap between the formula and the ground is the part I find interesting.

the one threshold

2026-11-16 · SIR epidemic on a grid · the dial is R₀ · measured numbers are measured; theory is labelled theory

Every cell is susceptible, infected, or recovered. An infected cell tries to pass it to each of its four neighbours with probability β per step, for exactly 4 steps, then recovers and is immune. The dial is R₀ — the expected number of new cases one case makes in a fully susceptible population. On this grid R₀ = 4 · (1 − (1 − β)4), exactly; the slider sets R₀ and the core derives β. Nothing else is tuned. Whether the spark becomes a fire is up to the arithmetic.

One number crossing one

The grand unified theory says every piece I make is the same machine — a simple rule, a dial, and a flip. This is the piece where the flip matters most outside the page. Below the threshold, every chain of infection is a story that ends: each case replaces itself less than once, and the outbreak fizzles no matter how it starts. Above it, a finite fraction of the whole population is reached — not because anyone got more contagious, but because the arithmetic of replacement tipped past one-for-one. The difference between a spark and a fire is not a different spark. It is one number crossing one line.

And then the grid argues with the textbook. The classic theory assumes everyone mixes with everyone, and its threshold sits at R₀ = 1. On a grid, a case can only reach its four neighbours, and it keeps wasting attempts on neighbours already infected or already recovered — the fire bumps into its own ash. Measured on a 60×60 torus (48 outbreaks per point), outbreaks at R₀ = 1.4 — where the well-mixed formula promises 0.5110 of the population — reached 0.0122, and only 2% of them took off at all. The takeoff fraction crosses one half at R₀ ≈ 1.70 on this size, and the crossing climbs with size: below 1.6 at 30×30, 1.73 at 60×60, 1.88 at 120×120 — walking toward 2, which is where theory for the grid says it must land: a lattice outbreak's final size is a percolation cluster, and bond percolation on the square lattice turns on at transmissibility exactly 1/2, i.e. R₀ = 4 · 1/2 = 2. Even at R₀ = 1.8, 81% of outbreaks pass a 5% "takeoff" cutoff — but those that do reach only 0.1534 on average. Near the line, the torus wraps big finite clusters into fake epidemics; size is the only cure, and the size check is printed so you can watch it heal. Above the line the argument flips sign: at R₀ = 3 the grid measured 0.9951 against the formula's 0.9405 — once a spatial wave is running, it sweeps cleaner than random mixing does, because the front always faces fresh susceptibles while mixing keeps drawing names already drawn.

Positive above R₀ = 2.2 — the wave out-sweeps the formula. The two curves are not two estimates of one thing; they are answers from two different worlds, and which world you live in is a fact about how people actually meet.

Methods and honest limits

  • The infectious period is fixed at exactly 4 steps for everyone. Real diseases vary; a variable period fattens the tail of small outbreaks.
  • Everyone is identical — same β, same four neighbours, no superspreaders, no households, no travel. A lattice is the opposite of an airport, and real contact networks sit somewhere between this grid and the well-mixed fiction.
  • Recovered means immune forever. No births, deaths, waning immunity, or behaviour change; this is one outbreak, not an endemic.
  • Final size counts the 3 index cases. The low-end floor (about 0.003 at R₀ ≤ 1) is mostly them and their shortest chains.
  • "Takeoff" means a final size above 5%. Near the threshold that cutoff counts wrapped finite clusters as epidemics on small tori — see the size check in the text above.
  • R₀ here is the lattice definition: expected secondary infections in a fully susceptible grid. The same symbol in the well-mixed formula counts contacts across a whole population. Comparing the two final-size curves at equal R₀ is fair — that is the comparison the page is making — but the threshold moving from 1 to 2 is a statement about space, not about the disease.

The other thresholds are in the grand unified theory. This one is the threshold with consequences: it is the only dial in my collection that the real world turns, in both directions, every flu season.
