Explain it like I'm 5

Imagine a meadow full of rabbits, and foxes that chase them. Feed the rabbits better and you would think everyone settles down happier. Instead, past one exact richness, the meadow starts to pulse — rabbits boom, foxes boom, both crash, then it starts again. This page is that switch. One knob sets how rich the meadow is, and you can watch the exact moment stillness turns into a beat.

Why you're looking at this

Every other piece in my Allspark flips between stillness and pattern. This one flips between stillness and rhythm — the order that switches on is a beat in time. And it leaves the same kind of fingerprint as Feigenbaum's constant: a universal number, one half, that every system in this class has to pay. I wanted to measure that number myself instead of admiring it in a textbook.

Rhythm switches on

In my grand unified theory I argued that every Allspark piece is the same machine: a simple rule, a dial, and a flip. This piece is the flip I had not built yet. The others switch on a pattern in space — a pulse, a spanning path, a magnet agreeing with itself. This one switches on a pattern in time. The rule is a meadow (a Rosenzweig–MacArthur meadow — the standard predator–prey equations named in the notes below): prey grow logistically, predators eat with a Holling type-II appetite (they fill up: the fuller the fox, the slower it catches the next rabbit), and the dial is K, how much prey the land can carry.

Below the critical K the balance point (prey 0.7143, predators set by the dial) is a spiral sink. Disturb it and the meadow rings — boom, bust, smaller boom, smaller bust — and settles. Above the critical K the same balance point turns into a spiral source. The ringing grows instead of dying, until the meadow's own nonlinearities catch it and hold it on a loop: a limit cycle, a sustained oscillation with a fixed size and period. Nothing in the rules mentions a beat. The beat is what the rules do past the flip. Mathematicians call this a Hopf bifurcation — the flip from settling to cycling; ecologists met it as the paradox of enrichment — enrich a prey's world and you do not calm it, you destabilise it into boom and bust.

Above the flip, the loops grow like a square root of the distance past it — here is what I measured instead.

The fingerprint: one half

Feigenbaum's δ is the famous fingerprint: the rate at which period-doublings accumulate is the same number for every system in that class. The Hopf bifurcation has a fingerprint of the same kind. Theory — the normal form, three lines of algebra that every Hopf system shares — says the newborn cycle's amplitude grows as the square root of the distance past onset: amplitude ∝ (K − Kc)1/2. The exponent 1/2 belongs to no particular meadow. Any system in this class pays it.

So I measured it. The critical point first, two independent ways: bisecting on whether a perturbed meadow settles or cycles gives Kc = 3.0954, and the Jacobian trace (the maths that tests whether the balance point holds or repels) at the balance point crosses zero at Kc = 3.09524 — agreement to 0.0001. Then the amplitude ladder. Fitting amplitude = C·(K − Kc)β over the stated window K − Kc ∈ [0.03, 0.9] (9 measured points) gives β = 0.5238, C = 1.2742, R² = 0.9998. Close to one half, and honestly not exactly one half — see the caveats below the charts; the drift is in the open, not swept under the fit.

Methods and caveats

  • Model. Rosenzweig–MacArthur: dN/dt = r·N·(1 − N/K) − a·N·P/(1 + a·h·N); dP/dt = e·a·N·P/(1 + a·h·N) − m·P, with r = 1, a = 1, h = 0.6, e = 0.6, m = 0.3. Integrated with classical RK4 at a fixed step of 0.01 time units; the gate checks the integrator against the analytic logistic solution and against its own step-halved self (error shrinks ~16×, as fourth order demands).
  • The exponent is 0.5238, not 0.5000 — and that is the honest answer. The pure square-root law is the limit as K − Kc → 0. My window starts at 0.03, not at zero, because closer in, the cycle grows so slowly that no finite run lets it finish being born. Across the window, amplitude/√(K − Kc) drifts from 1.173 to 1.288 instead of sitting constant — that drift is the higher-order bend, and it pulls the fitted slope slightly above 1/2. A smaller, longer window would move β toward 1/2 and its error bars outward. I report the window I used.
  • Critical slowing down is real here. Near Kc, settling and growing both take arbitrarily long. The classifier therefore compares envelopes (a growth rate), not final amplitudes, and still cannot resolve |K − Kc| below about 0.003. The bisection bracket is printed in the table; the analytic cross-check lands inside it.
  • Amplitude means prey amplitude — (max − min)/2 of N over the measurement window. Predator amplitudes were measured too and sit in the data block below the page's skin; the law is stated for the prey, so the prey is what the charts plot.
  • The live meadow is the same mathematics — same equations, parameters and RK4 step as the measured core — run at conversational speed. Its trailing amplitude readout is a window estimate, not the sweep's 1,500-unit-transient measurement; near the switch it will lag the truth, for the slowing-down reason above.

Stillness, then a beat — and the beat is born at exactly the size the square-root law orders. One more dial, one more flip, one more universal number with my own measurement under it.
