Explain it like I'm 5

Fireflies blink their lights. In the default mode here — global — every firefly feels the whole swarm at once: it senses the swarm's average flash and speeds up or slows down a tiny bit toward it, so they can all end up blinking together, like a whole tree flashing as one light. Switch to local mode and each firefly feels only the neighbours near it instead.

Why you're looking at this

I kept coming back to this one. Nobody is in charge of the fireflies, nobody counts the beat, and still a whole swarm lands on the same rhythm. I wanted to see the exact moment scattered blinking turns into one pulse — and whether it always does.

fireflies — phase-coupled oscillators

Each firefly has a phase that advances at its own natural frequency (about one flash per second, with a 12% spread). In global mode every firefly feels the swarm's mean field: the pull is K · r · sin(ψ − θ). Here K is the coupling strength — the same K as the slider below — ψ is the swarm's mean phase, θ is the firefly's own phase, and r (the order parameter) is a single number for how clustered the phases are: 0 when phases are scattered, 1 when all flash together. In local mode each firefly feels only neighbors within a 0.25 radius. Nothing is hand-synced; synchrony either emerges from the coupling or it doesn't.

Drag K up — past about 1.9 the whole swarm locks into a single flash; switch to Local and it can't.

What the sweep found (measured, not tuned)

The transition is continuous, not a sharp knee: r sits near the noise floor (~0.07) up to K ≈ 0.7, lifts gently from K ≈ 1.0 (onset near the theoretical Kc ≈ 1.2 for this frequency spread), and only crosses r = 0.8 at K = 1.9 — a threshold I imposed, not a feature the system has. At that same K, local coupling reaches only r = 0.424: radius neighbors form partial order, not global sync. N = 120, 120 s runs, steady r averaged over the final 20%.
