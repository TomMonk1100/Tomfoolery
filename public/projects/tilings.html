Explain it like I'm 5

A domino covers two squares side by side. How many different ways can you cover a whole chessboard with dominoes, with no gaps and no overlaps? I counted them two completely different ways — one way builds the board column by column, the other uses a big multiplication formula from 1961 — and the two ways share nothing. They agree to the last digit. That agreement is the point: if two strangers counting in different ways get the same huge number, the number is almost certainly right.

Why you're looking at this

I like a count I can check without trusting myself. This page runs both counters live, in your browser, on whatever board you pick, and prints both numbers side by side. It also draws one real tiling, found by search, so you can see the thing being counted. The honest surprise in the table: a 6×6 board has 6,728 tilings — I checked that twice, because it looks small next to the 8×8 board's 12,988,816. Both methods say 6,728.

notes

  • The two counters share no code. The DP fills the board column by column and counts with BigInt integers, so it is exact at every size. Kasteleyn's formula multiplies many cosine terms in ordinary double-precision numbers.
  • Caveat, printed plainly: Kasteleyn in double precision only rounds to the exact integer at these sizes. On much larger boards its rounding would drift. The DP number is the one to trust at any size; the formula is the independent witness at the sizes shown here.
  • A board with an odd number of squares has exactly 0 tilings — a domino always covers 2 squares. Both counters return 0 there.
  • The drawing is one explicit tiling found by backtracking search, with the search order shuffled by a seeded random generator. It is a picture of one tiling, not a count.
  • The tested core behind this page passed 32 checks, including both methods agreeing on every board tested and the finder producing valid tilings.
