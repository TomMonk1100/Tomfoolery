Explain it like I'm 5

A river does not flow straight for long. Around every bend the water runs fastest on the outside, so it chews the outside bank away, and slowest on the inside, so it drops sand there. The bend grows and creeps downstream. Grow one bend far enough and its two ends nearly meet — then the river takes the shortcut, and the loop it leaves behind sits still as a crescent-shaped lake.

Why you're looking at this

I wanted to watch the textbook story actually happen, not just be told it. So the river below is one rule, applied over and over: each piece of bank migrates at a rate set by the bend's curvature, with the upstream lag the real theory uses. Nothing is scripted — the bends amplify, skew downstream, and cut themselves off on their own, and the counters underneath are measured live from the line on screen. On the default seed the first cutoff lands at step 116. I checked that before I believed it.

the story the model is built from

Flow around a bend is not uniform. Helicoidal (secondary) flow sweeps surface water toward the outer bank, the outer edge runs fastest, and it erodes — that steep eroding side is the cut bank. The inner edge runs slowest and deposits sediment — the point bar. Erosion outside plus deposition inside moves the whole bend outward, and because the flow's response lags the bend, bends also migrate downstream. When two bends grow until the neck of land between them is cut through, flood flow takes the shortcut: a cutoff. The abandoned loop, its ends silted up, is an oxbow lake. (Definitions: U.S. National Park Service, "Fluvial Features — Meandering Stream"; LibreTexts, The Physical Environment, §18.5.)

The model's one rule is a documented simplification of the Johannesson–Parker linear bend theory: migration rate at a point is proportional to an upstream-weighted average of curvature — curvature here, weighted by curvature just upstream, decaying over about 1.4 channel widths. The lag is what turns pure outward growth into growth plus downstream travel, and it is why loops eventually meet themselves.

notes

  • What this is not: this is a stylised migration model implementing the textbook rule — migration rate grows with local curvature. It is not how real rivers are predicted. There is no discharge, no sediment transport, no bank material, no vegetation, and no flood hydrograph in it. Do not read a timescale off it: a "step" is not a year.
  • The model triggers a cutoff when two non-adjacent pieces of centreline come closer than the channel width. That stands in for a neck being cut through; it does not distinguish a neck cutoff from a chute cutoff, and real cutoffs usually need a flood this model does not have.
  • Oxbow lakes on the page fade slowly and stop at a floor so you can still find them. The fade is a drawing device — no timescale for real oxbow infilling is claimed.
  • Left running for thousands of steps the model river folds itself into shapes tighter than real floodplains usually allow (sinuosity past 7 by step 1,500 on seed 7). Real valleys, terraces and resistant banks stop that; this model has none. The interesting, honest regime is the first few hundred steps.
  • The gate behind this page: 13 checks on the core — sinuosity rises above its start before any cutoff, a cutoff occurs within a stated 1,000-step budget (it occurs at step 116), the channel stays a single finite polyline of the same point count after a cutoff, and the same seed reproduces the identical trajectory hash. All 13 passed.
