Explain it like I'm 5

The Sun is always blowing a wind of tiny charged bits at Earth. Earth's magnet is like an umbrella that funnels that wind toward the poles, where it hits the thin air high above us and makes it glow — green, sometimes red on top, purple at the bottom edge. One number, Kp, from 0 to 9, says how hard the wind is shaking the umbrella. Small number: the glow stays up near the pole, faint. Big number: the glow spreads toward the equator, bright and wild, and people far south can see it. This page is a dial for that number. Turn it and watch the sky obey.

Why you're looking at this

I wanted to know what Kp actually buys you. People quote it like a score — "Kp 5 tonight!" — but the number is a magnetometer average from 13 observatories, and what it does to the sky follows one plain rule NOAA publishes: the auroral oval's edge sits near 66 degrees of magnetic latitude when Kp is 0 and walks about 2 degrees toward the equator with every step. So I built the dial around that rule and NOAA's storm scale, and let the curtains be a drawing driven by them. Plainly: this is a visual model driven by what the real Kp scale means — not a forecast. It uses no live data and tells you nothing about tonight.

why those colours

Charged particles guided by Earth's magnetic field strike atoms in the thin upper air. Each gas gives back the energy as light of specific wavelengths, and the wavelength depends on the gas and the height.

coloursourcewavelengthheight
greenatomic oxygen557.7 nm~100–150 km — the common body of the curtain
redatomic oxygen630.0 nmabove ~200 km — the faint tops, mostly in strong storms
purple / blueionised nitrogen (N2+)band emissionsthe lower edge, where particles reach denser air

On this page the red tops only appear at Kp 5 and above, and strengthen with Kp — that timing is the model's choice, made to match NOAA's qualitative bands (dim and quiet at Kp 0–2, bright and active at 6–7, very bright at 8–9), not a measured emission threshold.

notes

  • Not a forecast. This page makes no network calls and holds no live data. The Kp you set is the Kp you get. For tonight, use NOAA SWPC's own products.
  • Kp definition — GFZ Potsdam, the IAGA-endorsed producer of the definitive index (kp.gfz.de; dataset doi.org/10.5880/Kp.0001; Matzka et al. 2021). NOAA SWPC publishes a near-real-time estimated Kp from a subset of magnetometers. Real Kp is expressed in thirds (5−, 5o, 5+); the dial uses whole steps only.
  • G-scale mapping — NOAA SWPC Space Weather Scales (swpc-drupal.woc.noaa.gov/noaa-scales-explanation): Kp 5 = G1 Minor, 6 = G2 Moderate, 7 = G3 Strong, 8 = G4 Severe, 9 = G5 Extreme; below Kp 5 is below storm level.
  • Oval boundary — NOAA SWPC "Tips on Viewing the Aurora": at Kp 0 the equatorward edge is approximately 66° geomagnetic, moving equatorward about 2° per Kp step, reaching ~48° at Kp 9. NOAA states this is approximate and represents averages. The page's model is exactly boundary = 66 − 2·Kp.
  • One honest tension: NOAA's G-scale text says aurora at G5 has been seen as low as Florida and southern Texas, "typically 40° geomagnetic latitude" — well equatorward of the 48° oval edge at Kp 9. Both are NOAA's numbers; they differ because aurora can be seen low on the horizon from roughly 1,000 km away (NOAA's viewing tips). The strip plots the oval edge, and the table's descriptions keep NOAA's viewing language.
  • Reference places on the strip: Fairbanks (~65.7°) and Anchorage (~61.9°) geomagnetic, from a WMM-based compilation at auroralights.app; the regional markers (New York/Idaho ~55°, Illinois/Oregon ~50°, Alabama/northern California ~45°, southern Texas/Florida ~40°) are NOAA's own "typically" geomagnetic latitudes from the G-scale descriptions. All are approximate; none is a surveyed city coordinate.
  • Colours — NOAA space-environment topics (Combs & Viereck): atomic oxygen gives green at 557.7 nm and red at 630.0 nm; University of Alaska Fairbanks Geophysical Institute: green from oxygen, red from high-altitude oxygen, purple/violet from nitrogen. Screen colours are a drawing — a monitor cannot reproduce a single emission line exactly.
  • The curtain drawing is a seeded model: ray count, brightness, coverage and motion rise monotonically with Kp through formulas in the page's core, gated by tests (315 checks: monotonicity, the 66→48 boundary, the G mapping, determinism, no NaN over long runs). Those parameters are artistic, not measured emission intensities.
  • Kp alone decides nothing in the real sky: darkness, clear weather, moonlight and a view toward the pole all matter, per NOAA's viewing tips.
