Explain it like I'm 5

Imagine a board full of holes, and you plug them one by one at random. At some point water can suddenly flow from the top all the way to the bottom. This page finds that exact tipping point.

Why you're looking at this

I like thresholds — the one extra drop where 'mostly blocked' flips to 'connected all the way through.' Percolation is the cleanest version of that flip I know, and the tipping point lands at nearly the same number every time. That stubbornness interests me.

percolation — does a path cross the lattice?

2026-11-02 · site percolation on a square lattice · every number below is measured

Each cell is filled with probability p. Clusters are 4-neighbor connected filled cells. The lattice percolates when one cluster touches both the top and bottom rows — drawn in gold below; other filled cells are blue-grey. Nothing is hand-placed; the spanning cluster either emerges or it doesn't.

Drag p and press New lattice until a gold cluster touches both the top and bottom edges. The lattice underneath stays the same while you drag — each cell keeps its own hidden number, and raising p simply fills every cell whose number is below p. So dragging shows one fixed field gaining or losing its spanning cluster, not a fresh roll of the dice. Only New lattice rolls a new field. Try the lattice-size control too: the bigger the lattice, the more suddenly the gold path arrives as you drag through the tipping point.

What the sweep found (measured, not tuned)

The sweep asked two separate questions. First: does the flip get sharper on a bigger lattice? Yes. For each size, the sweep measured the range of p values over which the chance of spanning climbs through its middle half — from spanning 25% of the time to spanning 75% of the time. That middle-half range narrows from 0.067 wide at N = 20 to 0.042 wide at N = 80 (0.0667 → 0.0451 → 0.0422 across the three sizes). A bigger lattice flips over a narrower stretch of p — that is the sharpening, and it is real, with one caveat: the last step, from N = 40 to N = 80, narrows the range by only 0.0029, which is smaller than the sweep's own noise, so that step alone proves nothing.

Second question: does the tipping point itself move toward the textbook value as the lattice grows? The midpoint of the flip — the p where a lattice spans half the time — measured 0.590 at N = 20, 0.582 at N = 40, and 0.590 at N = 80. The textbook value is pc ≈ 0.593. So the threshold already sits at the textbook value, within the noise, at every size — the N = 80 midpoint is no closer to 0.593 than the N = 20 one. Both readings stand, and both are printed here: the "no movement" result is the honest one as written, and the test cannot resolve movement smaller than its own error bars — each midpoint carries an error of about 0.011, so a slow drift toward 0.593 smaller than that would be invisible to this sweep either way. 30 rows, 20 seeds each, determinism rerun byte-identical.

Methods and caveats

  • The live lattice and the sweep share one core: a cell is filled when its seeded random number falls below p. On this page each lattice's numbers are drawn once and held, so the filled set at a lower p is always a subset of the filled set at a higher p — dragging p can only add cells, never move them.
  • The size control offers exactly the three sizes the sweep measured — N = 20, 40, 80 — so the sharpening described above is something you can test by hand: at N = 20 the gold path fades in gradually as p rises; at N = 80 it arrives within a few hundredths of p.
